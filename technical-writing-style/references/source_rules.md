# Source rules

The principles behind `SKILL.md`, ranked by authority for work documents. Quotes are short and carry page references. Bernoff's pages are ebook indices, because the ebook pagination differs from print; Zinsser's are from the sixth edition, and Strunk and White's from the fourth.

## 1. Bernoff, *Writing Without Bullshit* (2016)

The primary source, because it is written for business and work documents.

**The Iron Imperative.** "Treat the reader's time as more valuable than your own." (p. 9) Every other rule in the book, and in this skill, follows from it.

**Prepare, draft, revise.** Planning comes before writing, and the plan is ROAM: Readers, Objective (what they will know), Action (what they will do), and iMpression (how the writer comes across). "Business writing exists for one purpose: to create a change in the reader." (p. 108) A document with no action for its reader may not need to exist.

**Front-load.** Start with the conclusion and follow with the reasoning. The opening states the objective and the ask, and the title or subject line should work if it is the only thing read. Bad news goes on top.

**Write short.** Always edit, since no one writes a tight first draft. Set a word budget (under 250 words for an email), open boldly, merge repeated points, and cut surplus examples. Cover the core clearly and leave the rest for a follow-up.

**The three habits.** Passive voice, jargon, and weasel words. His target is a 90 percent cut, which leaves room for the few that earn their place.

- *Passive voice.* The zombie test: if "by zombies" fits after the verb, the sentence is passive. Reduce it as much as possible, and keep a passive on purpose when the thing acted on matters more than the actor. Wording that hides the actor without being passive counts too, as do "expected" and "estimated" with no one named as the estimator.
- *Jargon.* Use only terms everyone in the audience knows, legally defined terms, or one recurring term defined up front.
- *Weasel words.* An adjective, adverb, or noun that signals quantity or intensity without precision. Writers use them to skip the research that would give a precise answer. "Don't tell us that many people do something, tell us which people do it." (p. 71) Some qualifiers are accurate: an explosive is "extremely unstable." If exceptions matter, write about them explicitly.

**Be direct.** Use "you," "I," and "we." Own a recommendation in the first person rather than hiding it in a passive. Don't write "we" unless the reader knows which team it means.

**Numbers.** A number needs a comparison, a history, or a base. Show no more than three significant digits, separate percent from percentage points, and report the source, the date, and the sample size. For a causal claim, state the case, give the evidence, evaluate competing explanations, explain how to test the conclusion, and say what to do about it.

**Reveal structure.** Anything over about 300 words has a structure, so show it. Use at most two heading levels, bullets for three or more parallel items, numbered lists for sequences, bold lead phrases, and tables for parallel data.

**Editing.** Edit in levels, from idea to structure to line to fact-check. Word edits on an incomplete draft are wasted.

**Containers.** An email says who you are, what is happening, what it means, and what to do, with a deadline. A chat message front-loads hard with a little politeness and links a document rather than typing hundreds of words. A report ends with conclusions and recommendations, and its executive summary works as a trailer full of names and numbers; a section-by-section recap fails.

**Tone.** Business casual. No exclamation points, emojis, or profanity, and no cheery closings that make the writer feel better and do nothing for the reader.

## 2. Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (2001)

**Clutter.** Strip every sentence to its cleanest parts. Clear thinking and clear writing come together. Most first drafts can lose a large share of their words without losing information.

**Plain words.** Prefer the short word that is as good as the long one, and avoid fad words. Never write what you would not comfortably say in conversation.

**Qualifiers.** "Every little qualifier whittles away some fraction of the reader's trust." (p. 71) Prune `a bit`, `sort of`, `rather`, `quite`, `very`, and `pretty much`.

**People acting.** Replace concept nouns with people and systems doing things. Use active verbs unless no comfortable alternative exists.

**Sentences.** Short sentences should predominate, with an occasional long one for variety. Each sentence carries one thought.

**Paragraphs.** Keep them short, but avoid a run of one-line paragraphs, which is as tiring as one long block.

**Technical writing.** Build a linear chain of fact and deduction, with no leaps. Start from the one fact the reader must know before learning anything else, and assume the reader does not know what you think everybody knows. Define terms on the spot.

**Business writing.** People who work for an institution don't have to write like one. Turn process nouns into actions, cut synonym pairs and triads, and expand insider acronyms.

**Endings.** When you are ready to stop, stop. A closing recap is a warning sign.

**Not for work documents.** Zinsser's advice on surprising last lines, narrative leads, humor, and writing for yourself suits essays and memoir and stays out of this skill.

## 3. Strunk and White, *The Elements of Style* (1999)

**Rule 12, choose a design.** Planning is the deliberate first step of writing.

**Rule 13, the paragraph as a unit.** Each paragraph is one step in the argument. The first sentence usually shows the topic through its subject, and a summary opener used too often becomes a mannerism.

**Rule 14, active voice.** Make it the habit. Passive is acceptable when the word that belongs in the subject position is the topic.

**Rule 15, positive form.** Make definite assertions, and use "not" to deny, never to evade.

**Rule 16, definite and concrete language.** Back each general claim with a particular instance.

**Rule 17, omit needless words.** "...not that the writer make all sentences short... but that every word tell." (p. 23)

**Rule 18, avoid a succession of loose sentences.** A run of two-clause sentences joined by and, but, so, which, or while becomes monotonous. Recast some as simple sentences or join them with a semicolon. This is the main source for the skill's rule on varied sentences.

**Rule 19, parallel form.** Express coordinate ideas in the same form. Constant variation for its own sake is a mistake, so steps and acceptance criteria may repeat a structure.

**Rule 20, keep related words together.** Place modifiers next to the words they modify.

**Rule 22, emphatic words at the end.** This governs sentences. At the document and section level, Bernoff's front-loading wins.

**An Approach to Style.** Place yourself in the background. Write with nouns and verbs. Do not overwrite, overstate, or affect a breezy manner. Avoid qualifiers and fancy words. Use figures of speech sparingly.

**The dash.** Use one only when a more common mark seems inadequate. The skill bans the em dash outright as a house rule.

## 4. Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)

Orwell's six rules overlap with the above. His sixth matters most for this skill: break any of the rules sooner than write something barbarous. The style rules here serve the reader and give way when they hurt a sentence. The evidence rules and the hard bans do not.

His rewrite of a passage from Ecclesiastes into bureaucratic English shows why length is the weaker test. The bureaucratic version runs 38 words against the original's 49, so a length metric prefers it. Its diction gives it away: 42 percent monosyllables and 39 percent words of three or more syllables, against 86 and 6 percent in the original. Word origin catches what sentence length misses.

## House rules

These go beyond the sources. Keep them, and know they are ours.

- **The em dash ban.** Strunk and White allow the dash as a last resort, and Zinsser values it. We ban it because heavy em dash use is a strong marker of machine prose.
- **The negated-parallel ban.** Strunk and White endorse antithesis as a strong structure (Rule 15). We ban the `X, not Y` shape anyway because machine prose overuses it.
- **The rhetorical-question ban** in work documents.
- **The fragment bans.** Strunk and White allow an earned fragment, and Bernoff uses label fragments. We ban the verbless aside because a machine's fragments rarely earn their emphasis. A bold lead phrase on a list item followed by a full sentence is allowed, though plain sentences are the default.
- **"I recommend."** Bernoff allows "I propose." The owner prefers "We should" or "I plan to."
- **The checker's numeric ranges** (median sentence length, long-word share, nominalization density, the weasel budget of one, and the rest). They are advisories. Only the first-draft cut has a source: Zinsser says most first drafts can lose half their words.

Misattributions corrected from earlier versions: a per-document weasel budget of one, the "fifteen seconds" figure (Bernoff says tens of seconds), "get rid of the Latin" (Zinsser's wording is about long words of Latin origin), and Zinsser defending the fragment (he calls choppy one-liners "verbless wonders").
