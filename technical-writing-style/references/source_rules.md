# Source rules

The underlying rules in the authors' own words, from Strunk and White's *The Elements of Style*, Zinsser's *On Writing Well*, and Bernoff's *Writing Without Bullshit*. The guide stays short by keeping the reasoning here.

## Bernoff: countable thresholds

Cut 20 to 50 percent of your first draft.

The reader gives you about fifteen seconds, so the point goes first.

"If you use more than one weasel word in a piece of writing, you're weaseling."

Average sentence length under 25 words. Paragraphs of six lines or fewer.

The zombie test for passive voice: if you can append "by zombies" to the verb and the sentence still parses, it is passive. "The decision was made" becomes "the decision was made by zombies," which reads fine, so it is passive and needs an actor.

Fat words to cut: `utilize`, `leverage`, `in order to`, `prior to`, `subsequent to`, `due to the fact that`, `a number of`, `is able to`, `in terms of`, `the fact that`.

## Zinsser: diction and flow

Use short words that say what you mean, and get rid of the Latin.

Creeping nounism is stringing two or three nouns where one noun, or a verb, will do.

Every sentence has one job: to make the reader want to read the next one.

The last sentence of each paragraph should carry a surprise, "the crackle of a whip." This suits essays and blog posts. It is wrong for a postmortem.

"There's no minimum length for a sentence that's acceptable in the eyes of God." Zinsser also defends the semicolon and the deliberate fragment. The guide overrides him here, and the house deviations section says so.

Clutter is the disease of American writing. Most first drafts can lose half their words without losing meaning.

## Strunk and White: structure

Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one topic per paragraph, and a topic sentence.

Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.

Use the active voice.

Put statements in positive form. Prefer "he was ignorant of" to "he was not aware of," and "forgot" to "did not remember."

Omit needless words. `the question as to whether` becomes whether. `there is no doubt but that` becomes doubtless. `used for fuel purposes` becomes used for fuel. `he is a man who` becomes he. `owing to the fact that` becomes since. `call your attention to the fact that` becomes remind you.

Write with nouns and verbs rather than with adjectives and adverbs.

Do not overwrite, do not overstate, and avoid qualifiers. "Rather, very, little, pretty are the leeches that infest the pond of prose."

On the dash: "a mark of separation stronger than a comma, less formal than a colon, and more relaxed than parentheses." Use one "only when a more common mark of punctuation seems inadequate." The guide bans it anyway, as a house deviation.

## Orwell: why length is the weaker test

Orwell's rewrite of a passage from Ecclesiastes in bureaucratic English runs 38 words against the original's 49. The bureaucratic version is shorter, so any length metric prefers it.

The split appears in diction. The bureaucratic version runs 42 percent monosyllables and 39 percent words of three or more syllables. The original runs 86 percent and 6 percent. Measuring word origin catches what measuring sentence length misses.
